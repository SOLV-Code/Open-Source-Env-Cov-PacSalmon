### Introduction

The *Skeena marine growth project* uses archives of sockeye salmon scales collected from Skeena fisheries to study patterns of salmon growth and ecosystem conditions and how they relate to climate trends over the last 100 years. A key component of the work is to look for environmental covariates that are linked to the observed changes in salmon growth. The long time horizon restricts the type of information that can be used to construct covariate series, but also provides a much longer baseline for testing any candidate covariates than is used in many of other published analyses.

The main objectives for this study are to (1) Reconstruct marine growth trends for distinct age classes of Skeena sockeye salmon throughout the last century and identify potential periods of growth regime change, (2) identify dominant trends of growth among 13 populations of Skeena sockeye and assess population synchrony, (3) examine year-specific growth trends throughout marine residency and identify life-history- specific patterns of growth, and (4) evaluate time-varying and life history-specific responses to basin-wide climate variability and competition in the North Pacific Ocean.

To learn about previous research with the Skeena scale archives exploring a century of change in abundance, diversity, and freshwater growth trends, see the following publications by [Michael H.H. Price](http://www.michaelprice.org/research.html).

[Price et al. 2024.](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/gcb.17095) *Habitat modulates population-level responses of salmon growth to a century of change in climate and competition.* Global Change Biology. 

[Price et al. 2021.](http://www.michaelprice.org/uploads/1/0/8/9/108971119/price_etal_2021_portfolio_simplification.pdf) *Portfolio simplification arising from a century of change in salmon population diversity and artificial production.* Journal of Applied Ecology. 

[Price et al. 2019.](http://www.michaelprice.org/uploads/1/0/8/9/108971119/price_etal_2019_genetics_of_fish_scales.pdf) *Genetics of century-old fish scales reveal population patterns of decline. Conservation Letters.*
